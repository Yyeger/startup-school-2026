**Lecture on Pivoting: When to Change Your Idea**

- **Definition of a Pivot:** At its simplest level, a pivot is just "changing your idea." While famous examples like Slack involved huge shifts, for early-stage startups, changing assumptions and ideas should be a constant, lightweight process.

- **The Opportunity Cost:** The primary reason to pivot is that time is a finite resource. If you spend months on an idea that isn't working, you are paying an "opportunity cost" by not working on a better alternative.

- **Good Reasons to Pivot:** You should consider a pivot if growth is stagnant, you hate the daily work, you aren't a good fit for the industry, or you are relying on external factors outside your control (like mainstream VR adoption) for success.

- **The Danger of Politeness:** Many founders wait too long to pivot because they mistake "polite feedback" for traction. Customers rarely tell you an idea is "horrible" to your face; they just don't buy or use the product.

- **Shots on Goal:** Getting "lucky" in startups is often a matter of statistics. Pivoting allows you more "shots on goal." Taking several high-quality attempts at different ideas increases your odds of finding Product-Market Fit.

- **The "Uncanny Valley" of Traction:** Having a "little bit" of traction can be a trap. It is often easier to pivot when an idea fails completely because it gives the founders the freedom to move on without second-guessing.

- **Selecting a New Idea:** A good pivot should lead to an idea that makes you more optimistic and excited. Interestingly, "harder" ideas are often better because they are more inspiring and less boring than generic "ad-tech" or "affiliate" ideas.

- **Venture Capital Fit:** If you want VC funding, your idea must be "venture scale." This means it needs the potential to generate hundreds of millions in revenue, have high margins (usually software), and be capable of becoming a public company.

- **The Quality Score Framework:** Evaluate new ideas based on four criteria: how big the market is, "Founder-Market Fit" (your specific expertise), how easy it is to get started/ship, and early market feedback.

- **Avoid Founder Whiplash:** While pivoting is necessary, doing it too often (e.g., every day) causes "whiplash," which leads to burnout and sadness. You must find a happy medium between being obsessed with a failing idea and being chronically indecisive.
