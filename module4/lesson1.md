**How to Talk to Users (Gustav Alströmer, YC Group Partner)**

## Speaker Profile: Gustav

- **Current Role:** Group Partner at Y Combinator (since 2017).
- **Experience:** Former Product Lead at Airbnb; YC Founder (2007).
- **Core Thesis:** The best founders maintain a direct, two-way conversation with their users throughout the entire lifetime of the company.

## 1. Why Talk to Users?

- **Ensures Honesty:** Customers are the only stakeholders paying you; they provide the most unvarnished truth.
- **Deep Understanding:** Success comes from understanding motivations, not just data (e.g., Airbnb founders sharing personal phone numbers with early hosts).
- **Avoids "Scalability Traps":** Founders often hide behind "no-reply" emails or Google Ads instead of building personal connections.

## 2. How to Find and Reach Potential Users

- **Warm Leads:** Start with personal networks and former co-workers; they are likely to respond, though you must push them to be honest.
- **Cold Outreach:** Utilize LinkedIn, Reddit, Slack, Discord, and in-person events.
- **Targeting:** Search for specific job titles (e.g., CFOs or "Sustainability Leads") relevant to your problem space.
- **Outreach Tactics:** Keep messages brief, introduce yourself, mention a commonality, and ask for a 20-minute call. Do not over-explain the product in the initial message.

## 3. The Interview Process: Best Practices

- **Medium Matters:** Prioritize video calls, phone calls, or in-person meetings over surveys.
- **Listen, Don't Sell:** The goal is to listen. Do not introduce your idea/product until the very end, or not at all, to avoid biasing the respondent.
- **Build Rapport:** Ensure the interviewee feels comfortable enough to share pain points they haven't told others.
- **Documentation:** Take detailed notes or translate recordings into actionable insights immediately.

## 4. Effective Question Framework

**What to Ask:**
- "Tell me how you do [Task X] today."
- "What is the hardest thing about [Task X]?"
- "Why is it hard? Why is it important to your company?"
- "How often do you have to deal with this problem?"
- "What do you do to solve this today?" (Look for existing "hacks" or manual processes).

**What NOT to Ask:**
- "Will you use our product?" (They will say "yes" just to be polite).
- "What features would make this better?" (It's your job to design features, their job to describe problems).
- Avoid "Yes/No" questions; aim for open-ended descriptions.
- Don't ask two questions at once.

## 5. Identifying Problems vs. Solutions

- **Users have "Good Problems" but "Bad Solutions":** Users often suggest features to fix symptoms, not the root cause (e.g., Gmail users asked for a split-screen view because the app was slow, not because they actually wanted a split-screen).
- **The Excel Test:** If a user is currently using a complex Excel sheet to solve a problem, it's a sign the problem is real and valuable.
- **Sales Difficulty:** Evaluate if the audience is "easy to sell to." Startups are early adopters; industries like plumbing or contracting are harder to move off old tools.

## 6. Developing and Testing the MVP

- **Bucket Learnings:** Use sticky notes or software to group interview feedback into problem themes.
- **Prototype Testing:** Show users a design prototype (e.g., Envision). Give them a goal (e.g., "Try to book a room") but do not give instructions.
- **Think Aloud:** Have users narrate their thoughts while using the prototype to identify confusing language or UI elements.
- **Community Building:** Keep early interviewees involved via Slack or WhatsApp groups. Make them feel like "exclusive" partners in building the product to foster trust and rapid feedback loops.
