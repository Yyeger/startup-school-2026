**Paul Graham's Essay: What You Wish You'd Known About Startups**

- **Startups are counterintuitive:** Most of your business instincts will be wrong, and you will likely ignore good advice because it sounds "wrong." However, do trust your instincts about people. If someone feels "off," don't work with them.

- **Don't be a "startup expert":** You don't need to know the mechanics of fundraising or legal structures yet. Focus entirely on your users and their problems. Young founders often "play house" (renting offices, hiring people) while neglecting the only thing that matters: making something people want.

- **Stop "gaming the system":** In school and big companies, you can succeed by "tricking" the Proctor or the boss. Startups are as impersonal as physics—you cannot trick users into liking a product that doesn't work. Faking success might fool investors for a while, but it's a waste of your time.

- **Don't start a startup in college:** Startups are all-consuming and will take over your life for a decade or more. Age 20 is for "breadth-first" exploration—traveling, whim-based projects, and serendipity. You are actually more likely to succeed if you wait until you've gained more life experience.

- **You won't know if you're "up to it" until you try:** Even experts cannot predict which founders will be tough enough to succeed. Your performance in school is a poor predictor of your performance in a startup. The only way to find out is to do it—just not yet.

- **How to get ideas: "Just Learn":** The best way to get startup ideas is not to "think" of them (which leads to bad, plausible-sounding ideas). Instead:
  - Learn a lot about things that matter.
  - Work on problems that interest you.
  - Work with people you like and respect.

If you "live in the future" by being at the leading edge of a technology, the right ideas will seem obvious to you. The ultimate advice for a student: Just learn.
